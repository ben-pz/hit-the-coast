# Voice — how Hit the Coast writes

**Benjamin's brief (August 2026):**

> A tone of voice for the writing, in the spirit of LazyFPL — concise and
> witty, with a proper balance of humour and analysis. Not so witty that it
> tips over into being a bit much.

This is a guide for the Stories & Gear section — club stories, race reports,
beginner advice, route guides, gear reviews. It is not for event listings,
segment data or anything on the tracker. Those stay plain, because they are
the site's honesty commitment: a reader has to be able to trust a distance or
a date at a glance, with no tone to see through. Wit belongs in the writing
about the running. It has no business anywhere near the numbers.

## What LazyFPL is actually doing

Worth naming the mechanics, because "be funnier" is not useful advice and
"sound like LazyFPL" on its own just produces an impression of it. Reading the
three examples closely, the same moves show up every time:

**The joke is often the analysis, not a detour from it.** "Just as it would
be bonkers to judge a 100m sprint on the first two and a half metres" is a
joke and a genuinely correct piece of advice about small sample sizes,
delivered in one line. The Manager 1 / Manager 2 sketch is funnier than
"some managers overreact to good starts, others panic after bad ones" — and
it is the exact same point. Nothing here is comedy bolted onto analysis
afterwards. Cut the joke and you lose the argument too.

**One big swing near the top, then it settles.** The piss-splattered
Guinness Book of Records is the loudest joke in any of the three — and it is
also nearly the first line. After that the register calms down and the rest
of the piece just talks to the reader like a mate with something useful to
tell them. Front-load the big joke; do not try to sustain that energy for
six paragraphs, it will not survive the middle of the piece.

**Second person, always.** "Your attention will now doubtless be focused
on..." "You'll definitely have managers in your life who..." It is never
"FPL managers this week" — it is always "you." That is most of why it reads
as concise even when the sentences run long: nothing is addressed to a
crowd, everything is addressed to one reader.

**A rhythm of short, then long, then short.** "Six weeks out, my back went."
lands harder next to a sentence three times its length either side of it —
that is a Hit the Coast line already, not a LazyFPL one, and it is exactly
the same trick. Vary sentence length on purpose. A short sentence directly
after a long one is doing work.

**Running bits, used sparingly.** Waylon Lazy and the Lazy Scriptures exist
because a newsletter that goes out every week can afford to build a shared
world over time — a joke planted in week one pays interest by week twenty.
Hit the Coast doesn't publish on that kind of schedule, so don't force an
equivalent mascot into existence for its own sake. PZ×RC's own running
joke — the club being named after who its founders used to be, and everyone
knowing it — is already the real version of this. Reuse what's already
there before inventing something new.

**It never fakes a number for effect.** "2.63% of the season has elapsed"
is a genuinely calculated figure being used for comic precision, not an
invented one. The comedy is in the exactness, not in exaggeration. This is
the one rule that isn't a style choice — see below.

## The one hard rule

Every fact stays exactly as accurate with the joke in the sentence as it
would be without it. The humour can come from delivery, timing, imagery,
self-deprecation, or dry understatement — never from stretching a distance,
a time, a date, an organiser's name, or what actually happened on a run.
"Forty-four miles along the bottom edge of Cornwall" is precise; the writer
finding that funny or grim is a separate matter and free to be as dramatic
as it likes. If a joke only works because a number moved, cut the joke.

## What this looks like for Hit the Coast

The site already has a version of this working — "The ultra I didn't run"
and "How PZ×RC started" are both closer to the target than anything written
from scratch would be, because they are honest and a bit self-deprecating
without straining for a laugh:

> The name is a joke about who we used to be. We have kept it because it
> stops anyone taking us too seriously, including us.

That line is doing the LazyFPL thing already — it's funny and it is the
actual explanation, in one sentence, no separate joke required. The dial to
turn is not "add jokes," it's "let a few sentences per piece be as
deliberately funny as that one," rather than aiming for consistently
pleasant throughout. A piece with one line worth re-reading beats a piece
that is evenly mildly amused with itself for six paragraphs.

Material that's actually available to work with, without inventing
anything: Cornish weather doing something different to the forecast, the
tide catching someone out, gorse and granite, cattle grids, sheep on the
path not moving for anybody, the pub at the end being the actual finish
line, PZ×RC's own history, the gap between what a route looks like on paper
and what it's like at mile nine, and the specific indignity of any given
climb. All real, all local, none of it needs exaggerating to be funny.

## Worked example

The live opening of "Your first coast path run: nine things nobody tells
you":

> If your normal run is 10K in fifty minutes, your first 10K on the coast
> path might take you an hour and a half. This is not a fitness problem. It
> is what the terrain does, and knowing that in advance is most of the
> battle.

Already honest, already useful. Here's the same information with the dial
turned up:

> Your PB face is not going to help you here. Bring your normal 10K pace to
> the coast path and it will hand you back a time forty minutes slower and
> a new, humbling relationship with the word "ascent." That is not you
> getting unfit on the walk from the car park. That is just what the
> terrain does to everyone, and the sooner you make peace with it the
> better your first run out here is going to be.

Same fact — the pace gap, the reason for it — with one image ("PB face"),
one short punchy sentence doing the work ("That is not you getting
unfit..."), and a second-person address throughout. Nothing invented,
nothing exaggerated: still exactly the pace difference the original stated,
just delivered with more personality. This is the whole method — it is
never about adding jokes on top of the information, it's about writing the
same true sentence with more life in it.

## A working checklist

- Say the actual useful thing. If a paragraph is only there to be funny and
  a beginner runner would learn nothing from it, it's probably cut material,
  not a keeper.
- One proper joke early, not six small ones spread evenly. Let most of the
  piece just be well-written and warm.
- Second person. Write to the one reader, not "readers" or "runners."
- Vary the sentence length on purpose, especially right after the biggest
  line in the piece.
- Read it out loud. If a line needs a delivery to land, it isn't landing on
  the page.
- Every number stays exactly as true after the edit as before it. No
  exceptions, no "it's basically true," no rounding for a punchline.
- If in doubt, cut the joke and keep the sentence. A piece that is merely
  concise and clear is still a win. A piece that oversells a moment to get
  a laugh is not — and it's a worse trade for a site whose whole pitch to
  the reader is that the numbers can be trusted.
